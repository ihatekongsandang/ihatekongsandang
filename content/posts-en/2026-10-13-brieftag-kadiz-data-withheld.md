## What the original post claims (the poster's opinion)

- It says the military has drawn controversy by not submitting to the National Assembly the number of times Chinese and Russian military aircraft entered the Korea Air Defense Identification Zone (KADIZ) and Chinese warships entered waters under South Korean jurisdiction, figures it had disclosed until early this year.
- It adds that specific figures on North Korean infiltration drills against the South and on North Korean artillery shell support for Russia were also withheld, and that People Power Party lawmaker Han Ki-ho (한기호) questioned whether a halt in U.S. intelligence sharing has made it hard to track North Korean, Chinese and Russian movements.

## What reports confirm

- In response to the National Assembly defense committee's requests for data on KADIZ and jurisdictional-water entries, Air Force headquarters reportedly replied that "submission is restricted in consideration of preventing exposure of our military's surveillance capabilities and protecting operational activities," and the Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) replied that "answers are restricted in consideration of protecting our military's operational activities." Because such data had usually been submitted ahead of the annual parliamentary audit, the refusal was reportedly seen as unusual.
- Until early this year, the military had disclosed five years of entry counts for China, Russia and others; last year there were about 100 Chinese KADIZ entries, and about 350 Chinese warship entries into waters under South Korean jurisdiction, including 8 by aircraft carriers, according to reports. In April, the military reportedly submitted answer materials to the National Assembly on Chinese warship entries.
- On North Korean infiltration drills against the South, the JCS reportedly said "no unusual movements have been identified" without giving figures, and the defense ministry reportedly told the office of People Power Party lawmaker Yu Yong-weon (유용원), which asked for the cumulative volume of North Korean shells supplied to Russia, that "detailed figures are restricted as a combined South Korea–U.S. secret."
- Rep. Han reportedly said "not even disclosing the number of Chinese entries into our waters is subservient," adding, "Isn't it hard to submit the data because the halt in U.S. intelligence sharing means we can't properly track North Korean, Chinese and Russian movements?"
- A JCS official reportedly said the military decided not to disclose the written data because it could cause unnecessary misunderstanding and expose surveillance capabilities, and that it would explain in closed-door, in-person briefings.

## What we left out of the summary

- Whether the withholding is linked to restrictions on U.S. intelligence sharing is a question raised by Rep. Han; the military cited preventing exposure of surveillance capabilities and protecting operations. Reports have not established any link between the two.
