## What the original post claims (the poster's opinion)

- The thread asks, "They wouldn't even let us use the word 'mine'? Is this press control?" and shares a FINF card.
- The FINF card reads "Channel A, at the start of the incident, per guidelines: 'We couldn't even use the word mine.'" Its caption says a reporter covering the DMZ blast said in a broadcast on Sept. 29: "At first, we in the media couldn't use the word 'mine' either," "we were told to call it an unidentified explosion," and "from the next day we described it as a 'suspected mine explosion.'"
- The caption says the phrase "suspected mine explosion" first appeared in news reports on Sept. 22, the day after the blast.

## What reports confirm

- On Sept. 21, the day of the blast, the Joint Chiefs of Staff reportedly said that "three of our officers carrying out an operation south of the MDL in the western-front DMZ were injured in an unidentified explosion" (breaking reports around 1 p.m.). News reports confirm the word "mine" was not in the JCS's first notice.
- On the same day, however, Newsis reported around 4 p.m. that the officers were injured in "an accident presumed to be a mine explosion," and Seoul Shinmun ran the headline "DMZ suspected mine explosion" around 5 p.m. **The caption's statement that "suspected mine" wording first appeared on Sept. 22 does not match the reporting record.**

## What we left out of the summary

- The original does not say who the reporter was or which broadcast it was (the card names Channel A, while the caption only says "a reporter"), nor **who told them to use "unidentified explosion,"** and reports do not confirm these points. We therefore did not include the "press control" or "reporting guidelines" interpretation in the summary.
- If the claim is that no outlet could use the word "mine" on the day of the blast, some reports did use "suspected mine" that same day, as shown above.
