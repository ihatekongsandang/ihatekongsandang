Following a complaint that, in the same week, the government quickly drew a line against a U.S. announcement but did not respond firmly to a North Korean mine incident that seriously injured soldiers, we have set out the government's responses to the two issues side by side, based on news reports. Other posts on the mine incident (some may be available only in Korean): [UN Command finds an Armistice violation](/post/2026-09-30-2pro-unc-armistice-violation) · [President Lee warns against "baseless conspiracy theories"](/post/2026-09-29-sbsnews-lee-mine-conspiracy-remark) · [Democratic Party skips the Foreign Affairs Committee inquiry](/post/2026-09-30-rekor-foreign-committee-dp-absence).

## Alaska LNG — the U.S. announcement and the government's response

- At the White House on Sept. 30 (local time), announcing South Korea's strategic investment projects in the U.S., President Trump reportedly presented the Alaska LNG pipeline as a done deal alongside a Texas gas power plant and eight nuclear reactors, saying that "South Korea agreed to pay to get its tariffs lowered, and part of that money goes into this project." He also reportedly projected that the Alaska project could create 12,000 construction jobs and 1,000 jobs in operation. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick reportedly said "more than $50 billion" would be invested.
- The joint Korea-U.S. fact sheet released the same day reportedly said only that the two sides "agreed to begin a review" of the Alaska project, conditional on commercial viability and domestic legal requirements, with no investment amount.
- At an Oct. 1 briefing, Industry Minister Kim Jung-kwan (김정관) reportedly said he had "expressed regret to Secretary (Lutnick) that the announcement went beyond what was agreed," and President Lee Jae-myung reportedly wrote on X that "commercial viability must be a precondition." The government reportedly reaffirmed its principle of proceeding only if commercial viability is confirmed.
- Money Today (머니투데이) reported analysis that the one-sided announcement was a card to win over Alaska voters ahead of next month's U.S. midterm elections while pressuring South Korea, which could still pull out over commercial viability. It noted that Alaska's governor and a U.S. senator from Alaska attended, and that although Alaska leans Republican, recent polls reportedly show the Democratic candidate ahead.

## The North Korean mine blast — the government's and military's response, and criticism

- On Sept. 21, a presumed mine blast during a path-clearing operation in the DMZ on the western front reportedly injured three officers, one of whom had a foot amputated at the ankle.
- When the Joint Chiefs of Staff said on Sept. 28 that a North Korean mine was very likely, the presidential office reportedly issued no immediate statement. People Power Party leader Jang Dong-hyuk (장동혁) reportedly said the military had effectively admitted it was a North Korean mine and criticized the presidential office as being on "mute" during a grave security crisis.
- At a Sept. 30 briefing, the JCS reportedly called it "a clear violation of the Armistice Agreement," strongly urged North Korea to apologize and take responsible measures, and warned that "all responsibility for what happens next lies entirely with North Korea." However, the statement reportedly contained no specific countermeasures or timeline, and unlike in 2015, when loudspeaker broadcasts toward the North resumed after the wooden-box mine provocation, no countermeasures were disclosed. The same day, North Korea's Kim Yo-jong (김여정) reportedly called the incident a "self-staged drama" by Seoul and denied responsibility.
- In his Armed Forces Day speech on Oct. 1, President Lee reportedly described the soldiers as having been "injured in an unforeseen accident," and the People Power Party reportedly criticized that he "did not even use the word 'mine'" and that "this was not an accident but a clear North Korean provocation."

## What we left out of the summary

- The two issues are different in nature: the Alaska case is a diplomatic and trade matter over what Seoul and Washington agreed, while the mine case is a military and security matter involving North Korea. We found no news report directly comparing the speed of the government's responses to the two. This post only places each response side by side in date order and does not draw conclusions about the government's intentions.
- The government did not announce that it "will not invest" in the Alaska project. Its reported position is that it will "proceed if commercial viability is confirmed" and is at the "beginning a review" stage.
- The military reportedly withheld a final conclusion on whether the North Korean mine was deliberately planted or had drifted. We will update this post based on later reports of any government countermeasures.
